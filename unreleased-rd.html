Formlabs: Next-Generation Printer

This printer hasn't been announced, so I can't say much about the product. This page covers how I'm designing its parts for high-volume manufacturing: die casting and injection molding.

Company
Formlabs
Status
Unannounced (NDA)
Stage
Heading to T0 tooling
Processes
Die Casting, Injection Molding

Die-Cast Air Ducts

I designed two large die-cast air ducts. Each one spreads incoming air around a perimeter, gathers it into a thin, uniform channel, and turns it to direct it exactly where it's needed.

  • 01
    Why die casting: Machining would waste most of the stock, and bent, welded sheet metal couldn't hold a consistent channel around the whole perimeter. The casting comes in at roughly half the cost of the sheet-metal version, and it's thinner, lighter, and has no seams to leak.
  • 02
    Shaped for the flow: A rectangular duct with rounded edges so the air moves uniformly, held to a constant wall thickness throughout.
  • 03
    Even flow distribution: I prototyped several ways to spread the incoming air evenly before landing on rows of small pinholes around the base, formed by side actions in the tool.

Injection-Molded Filter Housing

I designed this part from scratch for injection molding: a filter and fan housing with a built-in orifice and pressure taps on the side, so the pressure drop across the orifice gives the flow rate. It also carries a mounting flange, standoffs, a gasket seal, an extra inlet port, and cosmetic requirements from industrial design.

  • 01
    Designed to be molded, not just printed: I worked with the team on pull direction and core placement, deciding which features become side actions and which need lifters, so geometry that looks impossible to mold can actually be tooled.
  • 02
    Printed first: Printed versions of the real part run in the current prototype builds, proving fit and function before committing to steel.
