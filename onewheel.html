DIY Onewheel

Right out of school, I figured a self-balancing board was just a gyroscope, a speed controller, a PID loop, and some motors. My job at the time had a Haas CNC mill, and I wanted a project that put it to work, so I built one. It rode, barely, and it taught me a lot.

Year
2019
Drive
Chain drive, two CIM motors from FRC
Control
VESC with built-in IMU
Fabrication
CNC-machined aluminum, laser-cut plywood
Fig 5.1: The board from the top, and the drivetrain underneath.
Fig 5.2: Riding it, more or less like a bull.

The Build

  • 01
    Frame and fabrication: The side rails started as aluminum extrusion from McMaster, which I machined on the Haas. The cross members are 1/8" by 3" aluminum strip, cut to length and drilled on the mill, tied together with aluminum angle. The deck is laser-cut plywood, and the axle is McMaster hex shaft, turned down at the ends to fit the bearings.
  • 02
    Drivetrain: Two CIM motors, 12 V brushed motors from FIRST Robotics that I could get for free, drive the wheel through chain. I went for the biggest reduction I could fit, about 13.3:1: a 9-tooth pinion, the smallest that fits the motor shaft, driving a 120-tooth sprocket almost as big as the tire. I laser cut the sprocket from ABS in layers and glued them together for stiffness.
  • 03
    A discrete chain tensioner: Backlash in the chain makes a balancing board twitchy, so the motors mount on three standoffs with several hole patterns, each slightly offset. Picking a different set of holes moves the motor and changes the center distance to the axle, which works like a digital adjustment: discrete steps fine enough to set nearly any chain tension I needed.
  • 04
    Control and power: A VESC motor controller with a built-in IMU runs the balance loop, powered by an off-the-shelf 6S4P battery running at 12 V. All the electronics fit inside the 3-inch frame height.

What Happened

  • 01
    A fried controller on the first ride: I made it about halfway around the building, turned it off, and it never turned back on. The likely cause: the VESC was built for brushless motors, and brushed motors need different current limits.
  • 02
    Rebuilt with a brushless motor: I switched to a single brushless motor, which gave me the torque to actually ride it without breaking the controller. It rode, but it was unstable and the foot sensor never worked reliably.
  • 03
    Where it ended: The mechanical side worked; what held it back was how immature the controllers and firmware were in 2019. Chasing that wasn't worth it for a board I just wanted to ride, so I bought a production one and took the lessons below with me.

What I'd Change

As built, it was a fun toy for a lap around the block, not a daily rider. To make it one, I'd switch to a hub motor: more torque, higher voltage, and purpose-built for the loads a board like this sees. My controllers were barely rated for what I asked of them, and that headroom is what keeps you off the concrete.
