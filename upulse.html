Formulatrix: uPulse

uPulse was already in paying customers' hands when its liquid level sensing failed, over-concentrating samples and crashing out their proteins. I rebuilt volume sensing around load cells and came up with the valve architecture that made weighing the sample possible.

The Problem

uPulse concentrates protein samples by pushing water through a filtration membrane, leaving the protein behind. It also does buffer exchange: adding fresh buffer from a second tube and concentrating back down, over and over. Every step depends on stopping at the right volume.

The product had already shipped to its first customers. I raised the alarm that the level sensing fundamentally didn't work and pushed for a return to the drawing board rather than a patch. Field units were brought back and replaced while we fixed it.

Finding a Way to Measure Volume

The hard constraint: the entire fluid system was pressurized to about 20 psi. That pressure is what drives liquid across the membrane. But a sealed, pressurized tube can't be weighed, because the lid seal pushes on whatever is holding the tube. I evaluated several ways around it:

  • 01
    Optical / laser meniscus detection: Proteins change the liquid's surface tension and cling to the tube wall, so the meniscus was never where the sensor thought it was.
  • 02
    Dual-reservoir pressure balancing: Inferred volume from how pressure equalized between two known chambers. Kept the tubes pressurized, but wasn't accurate enough.
  • 03
    Ultrasonic and other methods: Also prototyped and tested; none reached the accuracy we needed.
  • 04
    The insight: To grade every one of these methods, we weighed the tubes. The reference measurement was already the best sensor we had. The real question became: how do we use a scale in the real product?

The Invention: A Restriction Valve

The chip moves liquid with a diaphragm pump: open the inlet valve and draw liquid into the pump chamber, close the inlet, open the outlet, and push. Instead of pressurizing the whole system to create pressure across the membrane, I added a restriction valve on the return line after the membrane. It is intentionally never fully sealed: it closes with less force than the chip's other valves, so it always leaks a little.

On each stroke, the pump diaphragm, with its larger area, builds pressure against the restriction, and that pressure drives liquid through the membrane. At the same time, the controlled leak keeps sample flowing across the membrane surface and back to the tube, so tangential flow never stops. Pressure now exists only inside the chip, only during the stroke. The sample tube sits open at atmospheric pressure, which means it can sit on a load cell.

Making Load Cells Work

  • 01
    Buoyancy compensation: Once the tubes were on scales, buoyancy effects started showing up in the measured weight. We added compensation for them in the volume calculation.
  • 02
    Designing around drift: Load cells can drift over a long buffer exchange. In practice drift was rare and accuracy was ±0.5 mL or better, but for low final volumes we stopped 1 mL early as a safety margin and let the operator finish by hand. Overshooting costs the customer their entire sample; stopping a little early just means a short manual finish.

Results

  • 01
    From running dry to ±0.5 mL: The system went from blowing through its targets to reliably stopping at a safe volume.
  • 02
    Shipped in about six months from raising the alarm to the redesigned product going back out.
  • 03
    A platform, not a patch: Formulatrix has since built on the same architecture, including a 10-chip version.
